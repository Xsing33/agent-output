1 · Executive summary

If you read one thing, read this.

The single finding

Your homepage wedge — “the connections between them are” and “Not Another Tool” — is now the industry’s default pitch. Four of the fourteen competitors I pulled say a version of it, and one (GTME) says it almost verbatim. The wedge isn’t wrong; it’s just no longer distinctive.

What is distinctive is something nobody in the field has: production receipts. Every competitor sells a claim. You have named systems, published architectures, and numbers that can’t be copied — 318 calls classified, 90.8% of buyer pains unprobed, 11,000 accounts enriched at 93.9%.

That reframes the work. You don’t need a new identity, and you don’t need to build pages about “AI engineer” or “cold outbound email” — those are synonyms and mechanisms, not audiences, and chasing them plants you in the generic-agency crowd you’re positioned against.

What you need is what you already half-proposed: more indexable pages, split by problem instead of by title. The good news from the search pass is that this lane is genuinely uncontested. Every competitor page is a services grid — Outbound / RevOps / Data / AI. Not one of them has a page for account scoring, call intelligence, or intent-data replacement. Nobody to fight.

The three moves

  1. Keep the wedge, change the proof language. Replace “I connect your tools” with the thing only you can say: the systems run on your stack, unattended — here’s the architecture.
  2. Build three pages first, in this order: /gtm-engineer-vs-revops (weakest SERP, closest to money), /gtm-engineering-cost (buyer intent, beatable), /what-is-gtm-engineering (top-of-funnel pillar).
  3. Split the FAQ into spokes. You’ve written ~15 rankable assets and put them on one URL. That page can rank for one thing. Fix it by promoting sections to standalone pages and letting the FAQ become the hub.

4 · The five commodity claims

Everything you currently lead with is said by somebody else. Here’s the evidence.

1“I connect your tools.”

Vas: “I connect the dots.” · Trevor: “duct-taping SaaS tools together” · GTME: “disconnected tools” · Golden Ratio: “connect the moving parts” · gtm-engineering.io: “end-to-end.”

Your homepage says: “The tools aren’t the problem. The connections between them are.”

Verdict: contested This is the category cliché. It’s true, but it no longer separates you — it makes you look like the field.

2“You keep everything / clean handover / no dependency.”

Emilio: “systems built to run without me,” “then train your team to run it without me” · The GTM Lab: “You own everything at the end” · Golden Ratio: “hand you systems you own” · Growth Engineer: “Everything we’ve built stays yours” · gtm-engineering.io: “builds under your logins… no vendor lock-in.”

Verdict: table stakes Ownership is now the expected baseline. Say it once, briefly, and move on.

3“Not another tool / not another hire / not an agency.”

GTME, homepage: “Not another SaaS tool. Not another headcount. Not another agency that just runs campaigns.”

Your homepage says: “Not Another Tool.”

Verdict: contested, nearly verbatim This is the most urgent one. Your brand line is someone else’s homepage. Keep the phrase if it’s brand equity — but the page copy underneath it must stop resting on it.

4“Signal-based prospecting.”

Vas: “Signals · Research” · Lead Line: “Catch the moment a buyer shows they’re ready” · Golden Ratio: “signal capture” · Emilio: “Signal-based prospecting.”

Verdict: commodity Expected, not differentiating. Fine as a capability, useless as a headline.

5“AI-native / agents in the workflow.”

Yalc: “AI GTN engineer… agents they build for you” · Growth Engineer: “Claude Agents SDK, managed agents” · Golden Ratio: names MCP · Emilio: “agentic workflows (n8n, Zapier)” · GTME: “AI & Custom Engineering.”

Verdict: fully commoditized In 2026, “we use AI” is like “we use email.” It is no longer a claim; it’s a baseline assumption.

What this means

Your gut was right that the copy feels “too narrow” — but the problem isn’t that you’re aimed at too small an audience. It’s that you’re aimed at the same audience with the same words as fourteen other people. Widening the target won’t fix that. Changing the proof language will.

The distinction that matters

Look closely at the difference. Emilio has big numbers. You have specific, explained numbers — and a described architecture behind each one. A buyer who has been burned by vague consultants reads “90.8% of buyer pains went unprobed across 1,139 classified questions” and thinks: this person actually ran the analysis. How to deploy it

  • Lead every spoke with the architecture, not the claim. Show the pipeline shape (extract → connect → ship), then the number.
  • Name the trade-offs you hit. Gong’s transcript endpoint is POST-only, Apollo has two deprecated search routes. Naming the friction is proof you were in the build — and no competitor does it.
  • Use your strongest single stat as a recurring motif: 90.8% of buyer pains went unprobed by reps. It’s a leadership-level finding that creates urgency on its own.

6 · Their gaps = your openings

Five structural holes in the market. Each one maps to a page you can own.

1Nobody sells by problem. Everyone sells “services.”

Every competitor homepage is a services grid — Outbound / RevOps / Data / AI / Content. Not one has a page for account scoring, call intelligence, or intent-data replacement. Your problem-spoke plan has zero direct competition. This is the single biggest opening in the report.

2Nobody goes vertical.

All fourteen say “B2B SaaS.” You have production proof in logistics/3PL, manufacturing, and food & beverage — and your own ICP autopsy found ~50% of wins came from logistics/3PL, not the vertical the team assumed. A vertical page (e.g. GTM engineering for logistics/3PL) would have no competition.

3Most are vague about what actually gets built.

Trevor Fox describes capabilities. Vas describes phases. GTME lists fourteen service names. You describe architectures — a two-axis Fit + Signal scoring engine with STRIKE / NURTURE / QUALIFY / KILL verdicts, a remediation loop, a response watcher. Buyers who have been burned trust diagrams over adjectives.

4The field is Clay-heavy or strategy-heavy. You’re neither.

gtm-engineering.io is Clay automation. Trevor Fox is strategy and modelling. You build systems that run unattended on the client’s existing stack. That is a genuinely different product — lead with it instead of with “connections.”

5No solo operator with production receipts exists in the fractional set.

The solos (Vas, Trevor, Ted) have no proof. The ones with proof (Emilio, Growth Engineer) are positioned as small teams. “One operator, nine shipped systems, verifiable numbers” is an empty slot. Take it.

7 · Threats, plainly

  • “Not another tool” and “connect the tools” are taken. Your two strongest lines are the field’s default. Highest-priority language fix.
  • Subscription pricing resets expectations. $2K–$4K/month, pause anytime, is a much easier yes than a $25K+ build. You need a page that explains why a build outlasts a subscription.
  • Toptal commoditizes the hire query. “Hire a GTM engineer” belongs to marketplaces and competitor homepages. Don’t fight there; win the problem queries instead.
  • Emilio’s funnel is a better entry. Free outside-in audit → €1,900 blueprint → build. Softer than a paid diagnostic, and it makes the first yes trivial.
  • Clay owns the definitional query. clay.com/guides/gtm-engineering is the top result for “what does a GTM engineer do.” You can’t outrank Clay on brand — but their page ends in “buy Clay,” and yours ends in “you don’t need another tool.”
The counter-move to all four

Every threat above is a commodity threat. None of them can copy your receipts. Compete where proof matters and price doesn’t: the problem queries, with architecture and numbers on the page.

10 · Per-spoke positioning briefs

One brief per page: who it’s for, the enemy narrative, the angle, the proof to feature, and where it links.

/what-is-gtm-engineering — the pillar

Primary query: what is GTM engineering · what does a GTM engineer do
Reader: A sales or marketing leader who has heard the term and can’t tell it apart from RevOps or “using Clay.”
Enemy narrative: “GTM engineering = Clay + cold email.” (The LinkedIn course economy owns this.)
Angle: Define it by output. GTM engineering is the layer that connects systems of record (CRM) to systems of action (signals, scoring, enrichment, outreach) — and ships to where reps actually work. Explicitly reject the tools-first definition, then show the six-part anatomy.
Proof to feature: The nine production systems as a single map, with the portal of 40+ live reports as the “at the end” image.
CTA: Run the diagnostic.
Links to: every Tier-2 spoke + both money pages.

/gtm-engineer-vs-revops — the closest-to-money page

Primary query: GTM engineer vs RevOps · should I hire RevOps or a GTM engineer
Reader: A Series B leader deciding where the next hire or engagement budget goes.
Enemy narrative: Socio360 and RevPartners own it — both conclude “hire RevOps first, always.” Recruiters frame it as a hiring question only.
Angle: Steal the ownership-map format (it works — RevOps owns what is true; GTM engineering owns what happens because of it), then take the contrarian position: “RevOps first” is right unless the data model is already sound and what’s missing is building. That’s the case for the majority of Series B teams who have a clean CRM and no systems on top of it. Then show the build.
Proof to feature: The 78-deal ICP autopsy — the data overriding tribal knowledge. This is the exact argument that you build from evidence, not org charts.
CTA: Run the diagnostic — it tells you which one you actually need, and you keep the findings either way.
Links to: pillar, /gtm-engineering-cost, /account-scoring.

/gtm-engineering-cost — the second money page

Primary query: GTM engineer cost · in-house vs agency GTM engineering · GTM engineer consultant cost
Reader: Someone with budget authority comparing options. ⚠️ Not job-seekers — that’s a different query (“GTM engineer salary”) and you must not blend them.
Enemy narrative: Recruiters publish salary guides; agencies publish “fractional is cheaper than hiring.” Everyone frames cost as rate.
Angle: Reframe from rate to outcome per dollar, and what you keep. Compare three real paths — full-time hire (salary + ramp + tools + risk of churn), subscription service (you rent, you stop paying, it stops), fixed-scope build (you own it, it runs unattended). Include the honest version of why you’re more expensive per month and cheaper per outcome.
Proof to feature: “The system runs on your stack, not a platform you rent from me” + the recovered-rep-time maths (6 reps × 30 min/day).
CTA: Run the diagnostic to scope your fix.
Links to: pillar, /gtm-engineer-vs-revops.

/account-scoring — highest-volume spoke

Primary query: how to score sales accounts · account scoring model · lead scoring from closed-won data
Reader: A GTM or sales ops person building their first scoring model.
Enemy narrative: Thin AI-vendor blogs publishing generic “Fit + Intent + Engagement” theory with no data behind it. One literally uses your “Fit + Signals” framing already.
Angle: Show the actual build. Two axes (Fit + Signal), weights derived from closed-won deals, verdict thresholds (STRIKE / NURTURE / QUALIFY / KILL), and the separation-of-concerns that lets you swap data sources without touching the model. Include the weighting maths and the “Director of Ops is not a C-suite persona” bug.
Proof to feature: 78 closed-won deals; the ~50% logistics/3PL surprise; the $100–250K sweet spot.
CTA: Run the diagnostic.
Links to: pillar, /gtm-engineer-vs-revops, /intent-data-replacement.

/call-intelligence — your strongest proof

Primary query: how to analyze sales calls at scale · bulk call transcript analysis · Gong call analysis

Enemy narrative: Conversation-intelligence vendors sell coaching — one call at a time, human review.

11 · 30 / 60 / 90 sequencing

Additive only. You just revamped the site — do not rewrite it again.

Days 0–30 — ship three pages, wire the links

  1. Build /gtm-engineer-vs-revops — closest to money, weakest SERP.
  2. Build /what-is-gtm-engineering — the pillar that everything links up to.
  3. Build /call-intelligence — your strongest proof, narrow win.
  4. Wire internal links: pillar → spokes; each spoke → pillar + 2 siblings, with descriptive anchors.
  5. Convert the FAQ from a kitchen-sink page into a lean hub that links to the spokes.
  6. Keep the single CTA spine (the diagnostic) on every page.

Days 30–60 — measure, then add the money page

  1. Watch Search Console: which pages pull impressions, which queries actually surface.
  2. Fix whatever underperforms — usually title/intent mismatch, not content quality.
  3. Build /gtm-engineering-cost — buyer-intent, beatable SERP.
  4. Add structured data: Service on spokes, FAQPage where you answer questions, Person on the hub.

Days 60–90 — expand where it’s working

  1. Build /account-scoring and /intent-data-replacement.
  2. Start the blog feeding spokes rather than standing alone.
  3. Test the vertical page on whichever industry shows demand.
  4. Sharpen the homepage copy to replace the commodity claims with proof language.
Two rules that matter more than speed

One intent per page. The moment a page chases two queries it ranks for neither.
The internal links are the mechanism, not the garnish. Orphan pages don’t rank. Hub → spoke and spoke → sibling is what makes this a cluster instead of a pile.
